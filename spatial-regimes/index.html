Which method finds spatial regimes, and when?

The relationship changes across space. Most models assume it does not.

Ask a housing model what one more room is worth and it answers with a number: +$33k, everywhere in the region. Look zone by zone and that number falls apart — near the water a room is worth three times as much, and out in the cheap rural fringe an extra room is a sign of a big old farmhouse and is worth nothing at all. Spatial regimes are those zones. This page is about how to find them, and about a benchmark of 3,564 controlled runs that says which methods actually do.

The last line is the spatial tell. A correctly specified model leaves error with no geography, so Moran's I sits near zero; here the error is strongly clustered, because what the model omitted was itself a map. Note also what the error map cannot do: the two zones with opposite slopes are both dark, because error says where the global model is wrong, never how. Recovering the how is the job of the methods benchmarked below.

Inference

The reported effect belongs to no one

A pooled coefficient is a weighted average of opposing mechanisms. Its confidence interval can be tight and its sign still wrong for every zone in the study area.

Decisions

Interventions land in the wrong places

Policies calibrated on a global elasticity over- or under-shoot wherever the local mechanism departs from the average — and the error map shows exactly where that is.

Transfer

Models move badly across space

A model fitted where one mechanism dominates degrades when applied elsewhere, which is exactly the signal this benchmark turns into a validity check.

02 Why it is hard

The regimes are on the map. They are not always in the outcome.

Here is the trap most benchmarks fall into. Map the outcome and, in the usual setting, the regimes are already visible in it — so a method that simply clusters Y looks like a regime detector. Switch to strict process heterogeneity, where the intercept is dropped and every regime's slope vector is normalised, and the outcome map goes quiet while the regimes underneath are completely unchanged.

Four ways to model spatial heterogeneity — and the cell nobody had filled

Existing methods sort along two questions: is the local relationship between X and Y assumed linear or allowed to be nonlinear, and is heterogeneity represented as a continuous surface or as discrete regimes? Three of the four combinations were well covered. The fourth is what this paper is about.

continuous → discrete
continuous · linear

Geographically weighted regression

GWR and MGWR let a linear coefficient drift smoothly over space. No boundaries, no mechanism beyond a local linear slope.

continuous · nonlinear

Attribution surfaces

SHAP and GeoShapley maps from a nonlinear learner. Rich and local, but they describe every location separately rather than delimiting zones.

discrete · linear

Regression-coupled regionalization

2KModel and SKATER-Reg carve contiguous regions whose internal linear regressions differ. Clean boundaries, linear mechanisms only.

discrete · nonlinear

XAI-driven regime detection — this paper

Cluster a nonlinear attribution surface instead of the raw data, and the discretisation of regionalization meets a mechanism that need not be linear.

The XAI-driven family, in five steps

The idea is one substitution. Regionalization clusters observations by their regression coefficients; we cluster them by their attribution vectors — what a nonlinear model says each covariate contributed at that location. The framework is attribution-method agnostic; SHAP is the instance used here.

A gradient-boosted ensemble fits Y without being told the regimes.

03

Per-location attributions

SHAP returns, for every observation, one contribution per covariate.

04

Cluster the attributions

Locations whose covariates act alike fall together, wherever they are.

05

Regimes on the map

Contiguous zones, each with its own nonlinear mechanism.

Three members are benchmarked here: SHAP-KMeans (cluster the attribution vectors directly), 2KModel+SHAP (attribution-seeded micro-clusters, then contiguity merging) and SHAP-RecSplit (error-driven recursive bisection).

04 What we found

No single winner. A division of labor.

Across all 3,564 runs SHAP-KMeans leads on ARI and SKATER-Reg is the strongest classical method — but the aggregate hides the finding. Which family wins is decided by the process type: regression-coupled regionalization reads a linear mechanism directly off its own criterion, and attribution clustering takes over as soon as the mechanism stops being linear. 06 Cross-regime transferability

A validity signal that needs no ground truth

On real data nobody hands you the true regimes, so a partition cannot be scored against them. This is the substitute: train inside one regime and predict in another. If the partition separates genuinely different mechanisms, that transfer should fail — a strongly negative cross-region R² is evidence of real process boundaries, not a defect. Values are truncated at −10.
